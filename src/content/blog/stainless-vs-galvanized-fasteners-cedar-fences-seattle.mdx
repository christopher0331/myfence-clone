## Why Fasteners Matter So Much for Cedar Fences

Western Red Cedar is a premium fence material: naturally rot-resistant, dimensionally stable, and well suited to rainy Seattle weather. But even the best cedar fence can fail early or develop ugly black streaks if the wrong fasteners are used.

Industry guidance from cedar manufacturers and fastener experts consistently recommends **stainless steel fasteners for cedar and redwood** because of their resistance to corrosion and staining:

- Western Red Cedar technical guidance treats **stainless steel nails as the best choice** for cedar applications ([Premier Forest Products cedar fastening notes](https://premierforestproducts.com/pages/102.pdf)).
- Fastener manufacturers recommend stainless for tannin-rich woods like cedar to avoid black staining ([SPAX fencing applications](https://spax.us/applications/fencing)).
- Outdoor hardware specialists prefer stainless in wet climates and with copper-treated or tannin-rich woods ([galvanized vs stainless outdoor wood guide](https://www.eagleclawco.com/blogs/news/galvanized-screws-vs-stainless-steel-for-outdoor-wood)).

## Why Galvanized Fasteners Fail on Cedar

### Cedar, tannins, and metal corrosion

Cedar contains natural **tannins and organic acids**. These compounds can react with certain metals — especially ordinary or lightly coated galvanized steel — causing:

- Accelerated corrosion around fastener heads
- Black or dark brown staining that runs down the face of boards
- Reduced holding power over time as the metal breaks down

Western Red Cedar technical guidance warns that electro-galvanized nails and staples can deteriorate rapidly and react with cedar's natural oils, producing unsightly stains and streaks ([cedar fastening guidance PDF](https://premierforestproducts.com/pages/102.pdf)).

Building-science and research sources also note that:

- **Galvanized coatings are sacrificial.** Zinc slowly corrodes to protect the steel underneath; wet, acidic environments speed that up ([USDA Forest Products Lab fastener corrosion research](https://www.fpl.fs.usda.gov/documnts/pdf1988/hamme88a.pdf)).
- **Tannin-rich woods like cedar accelerate reaction.** Fastener makers specifically call out cedar and redwood as woods where stainless is preferred ([stainless vs galvanised outdoor screws](https://www.advancedfasteners.co.uk/stainless-vs-galvanised-screws-outdoor-guide/)).

In a dry interior climate, quality hot-dipped galvanized can be acceptable. In the Seattle metro, your cedar fence is repeatedly exposed to rain, mist, and soil moisture — and that combination makes galvanized fasteners a weak link.

## What Those Black Streaks on Cedar Really Are

Many homeowners call us a few years after a cedar fence is installed elsewhere, asking why their boards have dark stripes running down from each nail or staple head. The short answer: **metal corrosion and tannin staining**.

Here's what's happening:

1. Moisture penetrates around the fastener head.
2. The zinc (galvanized coating) and underlying steel begin to corrode.
3. Corrosion products (iron oxides) and dissolved metal ions react with tannins in cedar.
4. The reaction produces **dark, often black streaks** that bleed down the board face.

Wood–fastener corrosion research from the [USDA Forest Service](https://www.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/pubs/24353) and [Forest Products Laboratory](https://www.fpl.fs.usda.gov/documnts/pdf1988/hamme88a.pdf) helps explain why metal choice matters so much outdoors.

Important points for Seattle homeowners:

- The stains are often **permanent**; sanding and bleaching may lighten them but rarely remove them fully.
- Staining tends to be worst on **light, clear cedar** and visible face-nailed fences.
- Once staining starts, it usually **accelerates**, because the corroding fastener allows more moisture in.

Choosing **stainless steel nails and staples** from day one is the most reliable way to avoid black streaking on cedar boards in our climate.

## Stainless Steel Fence Fasteners: How They Work Better

### Corrosion resistance in wet PNW conditions

Stainless steel fasteners are alloys containing chromium (and often nickel). Chromium forms a thin, continuous **passive oxide layer** that protects the metal underneath ([USDA wood–fastener corrosion overview](https://www.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/pubs/24353)).

Unlike galvanized:

- Stainless protection is **integral**, not just a surface coating.
- Scratches or minor surface damage tend to **self-heal** as the passive layer reforms ([outdoor stainless vs galvanized guide](https://www.eagleclawco.com/blogs/news/galvanized-screws-vs-stainless-steel-for-outdoor-wood)).
- Stainless maintains strength and appearance for decades in outdoor residential use.

Fastener and fence experts consistently recommend stainless for:

- **Cedar and redwood**, to prevent staining and corrosion ([SPAX fencing guide](https://spax.us/applications/fencing))
- **Treated woods with copper-based preservatives** nearby, where galvanized coatings can fail faster

For the Pacific Northwest, stainless makes particular sense because the **long wet season** maximizes the time your fence spends damp. Regional climate normals from [NOAA NCEI](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/) show why moisture-driven corrosion is a year-round design issue here — not a rare edge case.

## Stainless Nails vs Stainless Staples: Use Cases on Cedar Fences

### Stainless ring-shank nails

Best for:

- Face-attached cedar pickets and solid boards
- Decorative applications where the fastener head is visible, such as [picture frame fences](https://myfence.com/fence-styles/picture-frame-fence)
- Areas where maximum holding power and withdrawal resistance matter

MyFence uses stainless ring-shank nails extensively on [solid board fences](https://myfence.com/fence-styles/solid-board-fence) and other styles where exposed fasteners need to stay clean and tight.

### Stainless staples

Best for:

- Securing welded wire, hog wire, or other mesh to wood framing
- Attaching thin slats where a smaller footprint is desired
- Hidden fastening in certain design details

Because many cheap staples are only **electro-galvanized**, cedar manufacturers specifically warn against them for Western Red Cedar. Stainless staples avoid rapid coating failure, early loosening, and corrosion staining around each staple point.

## FAQ: Stainless vs Galvanized Fasteners on Cedar Fences

### Do I really need stainless fasteners for a cedar fence in Seattle?

If you care about **appearance and longevity**, yes — stainless is strongly recommended. Cedar manufacturers, fastener makers, and fence professionals point to stainless as the safest choice for tannin-rich woods in wet climates ([SPAX fencing applications](https://spax.us/applications/fencing)).

### Are “hot-dipped galvanized” nails good enough?

Hot-dipped galvanized is more robust than electro-galvanized, but it still relies on a sacrificial zinc layer. In a damp, acidic environment like Seattle cedar fencing, that layer still corrodes over time and can stain ([USDA corrosion overview](https://www.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/pubs/24353)). For premium cedar, stainless remains the preferred option for exposed fasteners.

### Will stainless fasteners rust or stain at all?

In typical residential fence conditions away from heavy marine spray or industrial chemicals, quality stainless shows very little visible corrosion over time ([outdoor stainless vs galvanized guide](https://www.advancedfasteners.co.uk/stainless-vs-galvanised-screws-outdoor-guide/)). No fastener is immune to every environment, but stainless is far more resistant than galvanized on cedar.

### Does using stainless affect the structure of the fence?

Positively. Because stainless resists corrosion, it maintains holding power longer than galvanized hardware that slowly loses section as it rusts.
