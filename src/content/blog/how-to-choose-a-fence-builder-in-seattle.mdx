## Fence builder vs. handyman vs. general contractor

Not every person who can “build a fence” is the right hire. A true **fence builder** understands post layout, gate framing, material movement, drainage, slope transitions, and how to build something that stays square after a wet winter.

A handyman may be fine for small repairs, but fence construction has more going on than it first appears. Posts need the right depth and spacing. Gates need reinforcement. Fasteners need to resist corrosion. And if your property has a grade change, the builder needs to know how to step or rack the fence so it looks intentional — not improvised.

A general contractor can coordinate many trades, but that does not automatically make them a fence specialist. The best results usually come from a **Seattle fence builder** that builds fences every day, not occasionally.

| Option | Best for | Common limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Fence specialist | New fences, gates, difficult layouts, material-specific installs | Usually costs more than a handyman |
| Handyman | Small fixes, hardware replacement, minor maintenance | Often lacks fence-specific process and warranty depth |
| General contractor | Multi-trade remodels or broad site work | May not have fence-specific crews or detail expertise |

## Licensing, bonding, and insurance in Washington

Before you hire any **fence builder**, verify the basics:

- Confirm the contractor is registered with [Washington L&I](https://www.lni.wa.gov/licensing-permits/contractors/)
- Look them up on [L&I Verify](https://secure.lni.wa.gov/verify/) — active status, business name, and classification
- Ask for proof of general liability insurance and workers’ compensation
- Make sure the name on the estimate matches the license and insurance documents
- If anything seems vague, pause before work begins

A legitimate **Seattle fence builder** should expect these questions. If a company gets defensive about licensing or insurance, that is a warning sign. For consumer complaint and hiring guidance, see the [Washington Attorney General’s consumer protection resources](https://www.atg.wa.gov/consumer-protection) and the [Federal Trade Commission’s home improvement advice](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/hiring-contractor).

## Written scope matters more than a low number

A low bid is only useful if you know exactly what it includes. The most common fence disputes happen when the estimate is too vague.

A good written scope should spell out:

- Fence height and linear footage
- Material type and grade
- Post size and spacing
- Post setting method
- Gate count, size, and hardware
- Fastener type
- Finish details
- Demo and haul-away
- Permit responsibility, if any
- Cleanup and disposal

If you are hiring a **fence builder** for cedar or hybrid work, the scope should also clarify moisture exposure, board movement, and fasteners. For cedar-specific corrosion issues, read [stainless vs galvanized fasteners](/blog/stainless-vs-galvanized-fasteners-cedar-fences-seattle). Industry guidance from [Western Red Cedar Lumber Association fastening notes](https://www.realcedar.com/) and outdoor fastener research from the [USDA Forest Products Laboratory](https://www.fpl.fs.usda.gov/) both reinforce why metal choice matters outdoors.

### Materials and fasteners are not minor details

Seattle weather exposes weak materials fast. Ask about:

- Post material and footing depth
- Board or panel species
- Rail sizing
- Screw or nail type
- Gate frame reinforcement
- Corrosion resistance

If the company cannot explain why they use a certain fastener, they may not be thinking long-term. Rusted fasteners stain wood and shorten fence life.

## Process transparency: planning, grade, and execution

The best **Seattle fence builder** does not just show up and start digging. They should have a process for measuring, planning, and adapting to your yard.

Seattle grade changes, retaining edges, tree roots, drainage paths, and limited access all affect the finished result. A strong process includes:

- Site measurement
- Property line discussion
- Layout planning
- Slope and grade review

## Comparing bids fairly

When people compare fence bids, they often focus on the bottom line first. That can be misleading.

A better comparison asks: what is included, what is excluded, and how durable is the finished product?

Practical ways to compare estimates from a **Seattle fence builder**:

- Compare exact fence length and height
- Check whether demo and disposal are included
- Confirm post depth and footing method
- Confirm gate framing and hardware
- Verify fastener type
- Check whether permit help is included
- Ask whether the crew is in-house or subcontracted
- Review warranty terms in writing

A cheaper bid may use lighter materials, fewer posts, thinner hardware, or a less durable install method. That does not always mean the contractor is dishonest — but you are not comparing equal offers.

Also look at whether the company regularly works in neighborhoods like yours. A local **fence builder** that covers the region understands Seattle access, weather, and soil. See our [service areas](/service-areas).

## Red flags that should make you pause

Watch for:

- No written estimate
- No license or insurance proof
- Pressure to “sign today”
- Extremely low pricing with vague inclusions
- Refusal to discuss fasteners or post setting
- No clear warranty
- Poor communication before the sale
- No recent project photos
- A crew the owner cannot identify

If a company cannot clearly explain how they build gates, that is a serious concern. Gates are usually the first part of a fence to fail when poorly framed. For durable gate design, see [how MyFence builds gates](/blog/how-myfence-builds-gates-that-outlast-every-other-contractor).

A real **fence builder** talks plainly about posts, rails, hardware, and warranty — not just marketing language.

## Questions to ask before you hire

Before you hire a **fence builder**, ask:

1. Are you licensed and insured in Washington?
2. Do you use your own crews or subcontractors?
3. What fasteners do you use, and why?
4. How deep are posts set?
5. How do you handle slopes and grade changes?
6. What happens if the fence line needs to be adjusted?
7. Is cleanup included?
8. What does your workmanship warranty cover?
9. Who do I contact during the project?
10. Can I see recent similar jobs?

A good **Seattle fence builder** answers directly. A weak one keeps circling back to price without addressing quality, process, or durability.
