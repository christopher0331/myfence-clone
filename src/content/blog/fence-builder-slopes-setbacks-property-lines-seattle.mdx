## Stepped vs. racked fences on Seattle slopes

Seattle’s topography means most projects deal with grade changes. A good Seattle fence builder must choose between **stepped** and **racked** (a.k.a. raked) panels run by run, not just “one style fits all.”

### What is a stepped fence?

A **stepped fence** uses level sections that “step” down or up the slope like a staircase. Each panel is plumb and level, but the bottoms and tops form a series of drops rather than a continuous line.

A local fence builder may recommend stepped panels when:

- You want a crisp, architectural look on a visible street or front yard  
- The grade change is **steep enough** that racking would distort picket spacing  
- You plan to integrate masonry piers or heavier posts that need level panels  

Stepped fences can leave **triangular gaps** under panels on steeper slopes. A Seattle fence builder will often control those gaps with:

- Taller boards or infill at the bottom  
- Short retaining segments integrated with the fence line  
- Slight changes in post spacing so steps land at logical points along the grade  

### What is a racked fence?

A **racked fence** follows the slope more closely: the panel itself is angled so the bottom and top rail run parallel to the grade. This keeps gaps tight and creates a smoother profile on hills.

A fence builder typically uses racked panels when:

- The slope is **consistent** over a longer run  
- You care more about **privacy** and tight ground contact than a level top line  
- Pets or children need fewer gaps along the bottom  

Not every fence system can rack effectively. Many metal panels and some prefab wood systems have limits on how far they can angle before hardware binds or pickets misalign. A Seattle fence builder should know those limits for the specific product you choose.

## Setbacks: how close your fence can be to streets and alleys

Before a fence builder sets a single post near a street or alley, they should consider **setbacks**. In Seattle, setbacks are governed by the **Land Use Code** administered by the Seattle Department of Construction & Inspections (SDCI):

- Land Use Code overview: seattle.gov/construction-and-inspections/codes/codes-we-enforce-(a-z)/land-use-code  

Setback rules can vary based on zoning, height, and what you’re building. While fences are often treated differently than buildings, a Seattle fence builder still needs to think through:

- **Front setbacks** along streets and sidewalks  
- **Rear setbacks** along alleys  
- **Side setbacks** between neighboring lots  

For example, SDCI’s guidance for decks notes typical front, side, and rear setbacks in neighborhood residential zones, which can be a useful benchmark for understanding how close structures can go to a property line: seattle.gov/construction-and-inspections/permits/common-projects/decks  

On complex lots or where zoning has changed, referencing current standards or working with your designer is essential. Changes to neighborhood residential zones and setbacks are discussed in the City’s adopted ordinances, such as zoning-related ordinances available through the Seattle City Clerk at clerk.seattle.gov.

### Street-facing fences and right-of-way

In many Seattle neighborhoods, your **property line** may be different from the sidewalk or curb location. The public right-of-way often extends into what feels like your front yard.

That’s where a fence builder’s site research matters. We:

- Review official parcel data from King County’s property research and maps portal  
  (kingcounty.gov/en/dept/local-services/buildings-property/property-research-and-maps/property-research)  
- Identify whether your planned fence line falls in the right-of-way or on private property  
- Align posts so your fence respects public use while maximizing usable yard space

## Property lines: why a Seattle fence builder does more than “eyeball it”

In a dense city, an inch off the property line can matter. A Seattle fence builder should never rely on old fences, assumed lot lines, or neighbor memories.

### How property lines are determined

The City of Seattle and Washington State point to a few key tools and rules:

- **Parcel maps & records**  
  King County’s parcel viewer and property research tools help locate lot boundaries and legal descriptions:  
  kingcounty.gov/en/dept/local-services/buildings-property/property-research-and-maps/property-research  

- **Survey requirements**  
  Seattle Public Utilities explains when and how surveys are required for city work and utilities:  
  seattle.gov/utilities/construction-resources/land-surveys/survey-requirements  

- **Boundary disputes and agreements**  
  Washington law RCW 58.04.007 describes how neighboring property owners can formally resolve uncertain or disputed boundaries using documented agreements and recorded surveys:  
  app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=58.04.007  

A professional survey is often the cleanest way to confirm corners on challenging or older lots. A careful fence builder will recommend one if there is any doubt.

### Changing property lines and easements

If a previous owner adjusted property lines or created an access easement, those changes should be documented with SDCI and the county:

- Seattle property line changes and lot boundary adjustments are described by SDCI:  
  seattle.gov/sdci/permits/common-projects/property-line-changes  

Easements, such as side-yard access or shared driveways, can limit where a fence can go. Tip sheets from SDCI on side yard easements and accessory uses explain how these agreements affect construction placement:  
- Side yard easements and accessory structures (Tip #221): seattle.gov/DPD/Publications/CAM/cam221.pdf  

A Seattle fence builder should review or ask about recorded easements before finalizing layout.

## Call 811 and underground utilities: never skip this step

Even if you know your property lines and setbacks, you still need to know what’s under the soil.

Before digging post holes, we always recommend contacting **Call 811**. It is the free, nationwide “call before you dig” service:

- Washington’s Call Before You Dig information is available at callbeforeyoudig.org/washington  

When you contact 811:

- Utility companies are notified of your planned digging  
- Underground lines (gas, electric, telecom, water) are marked on the ground  
- You reduce the risk of dangerous strikes and costly repairs  

Any responsible Seattle fence builder builds 811 coordination into their planning—not as an optional extra, but as a standard safety practice.

## Neighbor relations, setbacks & shared boundary fences

Fences at or near a shared property line can raise questions about ownership, cost, and maintenance. While many fences sit fully on one owner’s side, Washington law provides a framework for **boundary fences**:

- RCW 16.60 addresses responsibilities for boundary fences between neighbors, including shared maintenance and potential cost-sharing when building on a joint property line:  
  app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=16.60  

A thoughtful Seattle fence builder will:

- Ask whether you’ve discussed plans with your neighbor  
- Clarify whether the fence will sit entirely on your property or be a true boundary fence  
- Suggest written agreements when both parties are involved in cost or placement  

Being clear about setbacks and property lines up front avoids costly, stressful disputes later.
